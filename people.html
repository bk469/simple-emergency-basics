Essential skill

People & Community

Share Skills. Share Work. Help One Another.

In a serious emergency, people may be the most valuable resource of all. A group becomes stronger when people share knowledge, divide responsibilities, check on one another, use resources fairly, and stay organized.

Know who can do what.

If people are gathering now

Hold a five-minute organizing meeting

  1. 1

    Account for people and identify anyone injured, missing, isolated, or needing immediate help.

  2. 2

    Name the most urgent threat: life safety, shelter, water, sanitation, heat, or medical care.

  3. 3

    Ask who has relevant training and who is physically able to help.

  4. 4

    Assign one lead and one backup for each active task.

  5. 5

    Set the time and location for the next short update.

Stay within training

Community helpers support safety and professional responders; they do not replace qualified medical, fire, utility, rescue, or law-enforcement personnel.

The essentials

Key Points

  • Account for people first — identify anyone injured, missing, isolated, or needing immediate help.
  • Assign one lead and one backup for each active task, based on real skills and training.
  • Knowledge should not live in one head — prioritize backups for water, first aid, food, heat, sanitation, and communication.
  • Use a buddy system for hazardous work — never cut wood, move heavy objects, or check storm damage alone.
  • Date and time every notice, and correct outdated or false information right away instead of letting rumors spread.
  • Verify credentials before assigning a new helper to a medical, electrical, or structural task, and never assign dangerous work just because someone volunteers.

Check on people needing extra assistance

Plans should include children, older adults, people living alone, people with limited mobility, hearing or vision difficulties, cognitive or emotional needs, essential medications, medical equipment, service animals, or transportation needs.

  • Ask the person what help is useful rather than deciding for them.
  • Assign a primary check-in person and a backup.
  • Confirm communication methods, medication, equipment, power, and evacuation needs.
  • Record only necessary private information and protect it.

Use a buddy system

Person onePerforms the task

Works within training and stays focused on the job.

Person twoWatches and assists

Monitors hazards, maintains communication, and gets help if needed.

Use buddies for cutting wood, moving heavy objects, severe-weather work, checking damaged areas, and other difficult tasks. A buddy system does not make an unsafe structure or untrained operation safe.

Keep information clear

NextThe immediate action or decision.
OwnerThe person responsible for following up.

Date and time every notice. Clearly correct outdated or false information instead of letting multiple versions circulate.

Hold short briefings

  1. What has changed?
  2. What is the most urgent need?
  3. Who is doing what, with which backup?
  4. Which supplies are running low?
  5. What safety issue must everyone know?
  6. When and where is the next update?

Keep operational meetings brief. Record decisions and assign a person to communicate them to anyone absent.

Accept help safely

Ask new arrivals what they can do, what support they need, and whether they have relevant training. Verify credentials when a task carries medical, electrical, structural, or other serious risk.

Give new helpers a clear task, a partner, boundaries, needed protection, and a check-in time. Do not assign dangerous work merely because someone volunteers.

Know limits and manage conflict

Know how many people can be sheltered, how much safe water and food remain, what sanitation capacity exists, and which medical needs can be handled safely. Seek outside assistance early when needs exceed capacity.

Stress can make people impatient, afraid, angry, or confused. Keep disagreements focused on the immediate problem, use the agreed facts and rules, and ask: What needs to happen now?

Free and inexpensive options

Low-Cost Solutions

  • A skills roster, contact list, and briefing notes cost nothing beyond paper, a pen, and a bit of time.
  • Rely on the skills people already have in the household or neighborhood instead of paying for outside training.
  • Look for free community-preparedness classes through local fire departments, libraries, or Red Cross chapters.
  • A plain notebook is enough to track resources — no special software or app is needed.
  • Practice a five-minute organizing briefing during a normal week so it costs nothing when it matters.

Avoid these

Common mistakes

  • Expect one person to manage everything.
  • Depend on one person for an essential skill.
  • Assume everyone knows the plan.
  • Forget people with additional support needs.
  • Allow rumors to replace verified information.
  • Use essential supplies without tracking them.
  • Assign hazardous jobs beyond someone’s training.
  • Ignore exhaustion or growing conflict.
  • Wait until an emergency to learn what people know.

Try this now

People practice

Gather the household, group, or participating neighbors and ask: What could you help with if normal services stopped?

  1. Complete the skills roster and note training limits.
  2. Name a lead and backup for six essential roles.
  3. Identify people who have requested extra assistance.
  4. Choose a reliable information point and update schedule.
  5. Practice one five-minute briefing.
  6. Identify the most important skill with no backup.

Arrange training or cross-training for the missing skill.

Before an emergency

Quick checklist

  • I know what useful skills participating people have.
  • Essential roles have trained backup people.
  • Responsibilities can be divided without overloading one person.
  • Primary and backup helpers will check people needing support.
  • We have one place for verified, dated information.
  • We can track essential supplies and daily use.
  • We know who coordinates each task.
  • We understand our limits and when to seek outside help.
  • Important knowledge is written down and shared appropriately.

Remember

One person does not need to know everything.

Knowledge becomes stronger when it is shared.

A calm, organized group can solve problems one person cannot.
