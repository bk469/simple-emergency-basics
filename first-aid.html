Essential skill

First Aid & Medicine

Stop the Immediate Problem. Get Help When Needed.

Small injuries can become serious if ignored. Basic first aid helps control bleeding, protect wounds, cool burns, spot danger, and keep a problem from getting worse until proper care is available.

Immediate action

If someone is not responding and not breathing normally, call 911 and start CPR. The steps are below. If an AED is near, use it and do what it says. Do not delay emergency care to keep reading.

The essentials

Key Points

  • For poisoning or the wrong medicine, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 (free, 24 hours). Call 911 if the person collapses, has a seizure, cannot breathe or cannot be woken.
  • Call 911 right away for signs like not breathing, not responding, severe bleeding, heart attack signs, stroke, choking, or major burns.
  • For severe bleeding, press firmly right on the wound and keep pressing. Do not keep lifting the dressing to check.
  • Cool burns under clean running water for 5 to 20 minutes. Never use ice, butter, grease or other home remedies.
  • Give fluids for dehydration only when the person is awake, can swallow, and is not vomiting again and again.
  • Keep a current list of medicines. Protect your supply from heat, freezing and water damage.
  • Never guess a drug or dose, share prescriptions, or use antibiotics without medical advice.

Respond to common injuries

Step-by-Step Instructions

CPR (not responding, not breathing normally)

  1. 1

    Call 911

    Put the phone on speaker. The operator can talk you through it.

  2. 2

    Push hard and fast

    Put the heel of one hand on the middle of the chest. Put your other hand on top. Push down at least 2 inches. Push about 2 times each second (100 to 120 a minute).

  3. 3

    Do not stop

    Keep pushing until help takes over, an AED is ready, or the person starts to move or breathe normally.

These steps are for adults and teens. For a child or baby, still call 911 and follow what the operator tells you.

AED (the machine with pads)

  1. 1

    Turn it on

    Open it and press the power button if there is one. Do what the voice says.

  2. 2

    Put on the pads

    Take the clothes off the chest. Wipe it dry. Stick the pads where the pictures show.

  3. 3

    Stand clear

    Do not touch the person while the AED checks. If it says to give a shock, make sure no one is touching the person, then press the button.

  4. 4

    Keep going

    When the AED says so, start pushing on the chest again. Follow the voice until help arrives.

An AED only gives a shock if the person needs one. It is safe for anyone to use.

Choking

  1. 1

    Check

    If the person cannot cough, speak or breathe, tell someone to call 911.

  2. 2

    Back blows

    Lean the person forward. Hit 5 times between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.

  3. 3

    Belly thrusts

    Stand behind the person. Make a fist just above the belly button. Give 5 quick thrusts inward and up.

  4. 4

    Repeat

    Keep giving 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out or the person can cough, speak or breathe.

  5. 5

    If the person goes limp

    Lay them on the floor and start CPR. Each time you open the mouth, look for the object. Take it out only if you can see it. Do not sweep a finger blindly.

Baby under 1 year: lay the baby face down along your forearm. Give 5 back blows, then turn the baby over and give 5 chest thrusts with 2 fingers. Do not use belly thrusts on a baby. Call 911.

Heart attack signs

Call 911 right away. Do not wait to see if it passes. Do not drive yourself. Sit down and rest. Take aspirin only if the 911 operator tells you to.

  • Pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain in the middle of the chest.
  • Pain or discomfort in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw or belly.
  • Hard to breathe, with or without chest discomfort.
  • Cold sweat, feeling sick to the stomach, or feeling faint.
  • Women may feel more tired or sick, or have back or jaw pain, with little chest pain.

Stroke signs (BE FAST)

Call 911 even if the signs go away. Note the time the signs started. Do not wait to see if it gets better.

  • Balance: sudden loss of balance or trouble walking.
  • Eyes: sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes.
  • Face: one side of the face droops when the person smiles.
  • Arm: one arm is weak or numb, or drifts down when both are raised.
  • Speech: slurred, strange, or hard to understand.
  • Time: call 911 right away.

Poison or the wrong medicine

  1. 1

    Check the person

    If the person collapsed, had a seizure, cannot breathe or cannot be woken, call 911.

  2. 2

    Call Poison Help

    Call 1-800-222-1222. It is free and open 24 hours a day. Do not wait for signs of poisoning.

  3. 3

    Keep the container

    Keep the bottle, package or plant nearby. The label has important information. Tell them if the person has thrown up.

Carbon monoxide poisoning: get everyone outside into fresh air, then call 911.

Heat stroke

Call 911. Move the person to a cooler place. Cool them with cool wet cloths or a cool bath. Do not give a confused person anything to drink.

  • Confusion, slurred speech, passing out, or a seizure.
  • Very hot skin, dry or damp, and a fast pulse.

Severe bleeding

  1. 1

    Make it safe

    Check the scene, call emergency services, and put on disposable gloves if available.

  2. 2

    Find the source

    Expose the wound enough to see where the blood is coming from. Do not remove an embedded object.

  3. 3

    Apply pressure

    Put clean gauze or cloth directly over the wound and press firmly and steadily.

  4. 4

    Keep pressure

    Do not repeatedly lift the original dressing to check. Maintain direct pressure until bleeding stops, trained help takes over, or the scene becomes unsafe.

  5. 5

    Use trained skills

    For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, use a manufactured tourniquet if trained. Note the application time and do not loosen it.

Severe bleeding that will not stop, or that spurts, needs emergency medical treatment right away.

Small cuts and wounds

  1. 1

    Clean hands

    Wash your hands and use gloves when available.

  2. 2

    Control bleeding

    Use gentle direct pressure with clean gauze.

  3. 3

    Rinse and cover

    Rinse with clean water, remove only obvious surface dirt that comes away safely, and apply a clean dressing.

  4. 4

    Monitor

    Keep the wound clean and watch it over the following days.

  • Increasing redness
  • Swelling
  • Warmth
  • Pus
  • Increasing pain
  • Fever

Get medical care for deep or very dirty wounds, animal bites, bleeding you cannot stop, a body part you cannot move or feel normally, or signs of infection. Ask a clinician about tetanus protection when it is needed.

Minor thermal burns

  1. 1

    Stop the burning

    Move away from the heat source and make the scene safe.

  2. 2

    Cool with water

    Place the burn under clean, cool running water for 5 to 20 minutes. Do not use ice.

  3. 3

    Remove loose items

    Remove jewelry or loose clothing near the burn unless it is stuck to the skin.

  4. 4

    Protect loosely

    Use a clean, dry, non-adhesive dressing if needed.

Call 911 for major burns, and for any chemical or electrical burn. Get urgent care for deep burns, large burns, or burns of the face, mouth, neck, hands, feet, joints or groin.

Chemical burn: brush off any dry powder, then flush with cool running water for at least 15 minutes. Electrical burn: make sure the power is off before you touch the person.

  • Apply ice directly.
  • Put butter, grease, toothpaste, petroleum jelly or other home remedies on a burn.
  • Pull off clothing that is stuck to burned skin.
  • Break blisters.

Sprains and strains

Stop the activity, protect the injury, use a wrapped cold pack for short periods, raise it if that is comfortable, and avoid putting weight on a badly hurt joint.

Get medical care for severe pain, a bent or odd shape, not being able to use the arm or leg, numbness, loss of blood flow, or if you think a bone may be broken.

Dehydration

  • Thirst
  • Dry mouth
  • Weakness
  • Dizziness
  • Dark urine
  • Reduced urination
  • Confusion
  • Fainting

Give safe fluids only when the person is awake, can swallow, and is not vomiting again and again. Severe dehydration, confusion, fainting, or not being able to keep fluids down needs medical care.

What you need

Supplies & Options

Personal medications

Talk with a doctor or pharmacist about the right emergency supply. Keep a current medicine list with the drug name, what it is for, dose, timing, allergies, medical conditions, pharmacy and prescriber.

Protect the supply

Keep medicines in their original labeled containers, dry, and within the temperature range on the label. Use a waterproof container if flooding or water damage is possible.

Medicines that need refrigeration

Some medicines, including certain insulin and liquid medicines, lose strength outside the temperature range they need. Ask the pharmacist or maker exactly how long each product can stay unrefrigerated.

  • I know the storage temperature on the label and how long it can be out of it.
  • I have a cooler and cold packs that are right for the medicine.
  • I can keep the medicine from freezing or touching loose ice.
  • I know where and how to get a replacement.

Do not guess whether a temperature-sensitive medicine is safe. Contact a pharmacist, health professional or the maker for instructions on that product.

Basic over-the-counter medicines

  • Pain and fever medicine
  • Antihistamine
  • Antacid
  • Antidiarrheal medicine
  • Oral rehydration packets
  • Other familiar household medicines

Read and follow labels. Check the active ingredients so you do not double a dose by accident. Think about age, allergies, pregnancy, medical conditions and drug interactions. Keep all medicine away from children.

What about antibiotics?

Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections, not every illness. Do not use leftovers casually, share prescriptions, guess the drug or dose, or use antibiotics for viral illnesses.

A doctor or other health professional should choose and guide the use of any antibiotic in an emergency plan.

Identify essential medical needs

  • Insulin
  • Heart medicine
  • Seizure medicine
  • Blood thinners
  • Asthma inhalers
  • Epinephrine auto-injectors
  • Oxygen
  • Mobility equipment
  • Hearing aids and batteries
  • Other essential equipment
A community first-aid kit cannot replace someone’s essential daily medicine.

Free and inexpensive options

Low-Cost Solutions

  • A bag of frozen vegetables, or ice wrapped in a cloth, works as a cold pack for sprains. You do not need to buy one.
  • Clean water that has been boiled and cooled can be used instead of sterile saline to rinse minor wounds.
  • Ask a pharmacist ahead of time about a simple homemade oral-rehydration recipe as a backup to store-bought packets.
  • A basic drugstore first-aid kit is cheap and covers most of the supplies listed here.
  • Local fire departments, Red Cross chapters and community centers often offer free or low-cost first-aid, CPR and AED training.

Avoid these

Common mistakes

  • Wait to call in a life-threatening emergency.
  • Keep taking pressure off a severely bleeding wound.
  • Put ice, butter, or grease on burns.
  • Move someone who may have a spine injury when you do not need to.
  • Give medicine without knowing what it is or checking the label.
  • Share prescription medicine.
  • Forget daily medications and medical equipment.
  • Wait until medicine runs out to get a replacement.

Try this now

First-aid practice

Open the kit instead of just looking at the box.

  1. Find the gloves, gauze, and wound dressings.
  2. Say how you would call for help and control bleeding.
  3. Check every medicine and sterile item for damage or an expired date.
  4. Find the paper medication and allergy lists.
  5. Find the nearest AED and who knows CPR.
  6. List each household member’s essential medical needs.

Replace missing or expired supplies. Then schedule hands-on first-aid, CPR, AED and bleeding-control training.

Before an emergency

Quick checklist

  • I have a first-aid kit I can use and check regularly.
  • I know how to call for help and control serious bleeding.
  • I know basic wound and burn care.
  • I have medicine, allergy, condition and contact lists.
  • Essential prescriptions and equipment are in my plan.
  • I know exactly how to store medicines that need to be kept cold.
  • I know where the nearest AED is.
  • At least one person has current first-aid and CPR training.

Remember

The first job of first aid is not to become a doctor.
