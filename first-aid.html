First Aid & Medicine

Stop the Immediate Problem. Get Help When Needed.

Small injuries can become serious when ignored. Basic first aid helps control bleeding, protect wounds, cool burns, recognize danger, and keep a problem from becoming worse until proper care is available.

The essentials

Key Points

  • Call 911 immediately for signs like not breathing, unresponsiveness, severe bleeding, chest pain, stroke, or major burns.
  • For severe bleeding, apply firm direct pressure and keep it there — do not repeatedly lift the dressing to check.
  • Cool burns under clean running water for 5 to 20 minutes. Never use ice, butter, grease, or other home remedies.
  • Give fluids for dehydration only when the person is awake, able to swallow, and not vomiting repeatedly.
  • Keep an up-to-date medication list and protect the supply from heat, freezing, and water damage.
  • Never guess a drug or dose, share prescriptions, or use antibiotics without medical guidance.

Respond to common injuries

Step-by-Step Instructions

Severe bleeding

  1. 1

    Make it safe

    Check the scene, call emergency services, and put on disposable gloves if available.

  2. 2

    Find the source

    Expose the wound enough to see where the blood is coming from. Do not remove an embedded object.

  3. 3

    Apply pressure

    Put clean gauze or cloth directly over the wound and press firmly and steadily.

  4. 4

    Keep pressure

    Do not repeatedly lift the original dressing to check. Maintain direct pressure until bleeding stops, trained help takes over, or the scene becomes unsafe.

  5. 5

    Use trained skills

    For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, use a manufactured tourniquet if trained. Note the application time and do not loosen it.

Severe continuous or spurting bleeding requires immediate emergency medical treatment.

Small cuts and wounds

  1. 1

    Clean hands

    Wash your hands and use gloves when available.

  2. 2

    Control bleeding

    Use gentle direct pressure with clean gauze.

  3. 3

    Rinse and cover

    Rinse with clean water, remove only obvious surface dirt that comes away safely, and apply a clean dressing.

  4. 4

    Monitor

    Keep the wound clean and watch it over the following days.

  • Increasing redness
  • Swelling
  • Warmth
  • Pus
  • Increasing pain
  • Fever

Seek medical care for deep or badly contaminated wounds, animal bites, uncontrolled bleeding, loss of function, or signs of infection. Ask a clinician about tetanus protection when appropriate.

Minor thermal burns

  1. 1

    Stop the burning

    Move away from the heat source and make the scene safe.

  2. 2

    Cool with water

    Place the burn under clean, cool running water for 5 to 20 minutes. Do not use ice.

  3. 3

    Remove loose items

    Remove jewelry or loose clothing near the burn unless it is stuck to the skin.

  4. 4

    Protect loosely

    Use a clean, dry, non-adhesive dressing if needed.

Call emergency services for major burns. Seek urgent evaluation for chemical or electrical burns, deep burns, large areas, or burns of the face, mouth, neck, hands, feet, joints, or groin.

  • Apply ice directly.
  • Apply butter, grease, toothpaste, petroleum jelly, or other home remedies.
  • Pull away clothing stuck to burned skin.
  • Break blisters.

Sprains and strains

Stop the activity, protect the injury, use a wrapped cold pack for short periods, elevate it if comfortable, and avoid unnecessary weight on a badly injured joint.

Seek medical care for severe pain, deformity, inability to use the limb, numbness, loss of circulation, or concern for a fracture.

Dehydration

  • Thirst
  • Dry mouth
  • Weakness
  • Dizziness
  • Dark urine
  • Reduced urination
  • Confusion
  • Fainting

Give safe fluids only when the person is awake, able to swallow, and not vomiting repeatedly. Severe dehydration, confusion, fainting, or inability to keep fluids down requires medical attention.

What you need

Supplies & Options

Personal medications

Talk with a doctor or pharmacist about an appropriate emergency supply. Keep an up-to-date medication list with the drug name, purpose, dose, timing, allergies, medical conditions, pharmacy, and prescriber.

Protect the supply

Keep medicines in original labeled containers, stored dry and within the temperature limits on the label. Use a waterproof container if flooding or water damage is possible.

Medicines that need refrigeration

Some medicines, including certain insulin and liquid medicines, lose strength outside their required temperature range. Ask the pharmacist or manufacturer exactly how long each product can remain unrefrigerated.

  • I know the labeled storage temperature and allowable time outside it.
  • I have a cooler and cold packs suitable for the medicine.
  • I can prevent the medicine from freezing or touching loose ice.
  • I know where and how to obtain a replacement.

Do not guess whether a temperature-sensitive medicine is safe. Contact a pharmacist, healthcare professional, or manufacturer for product-specific instructions.

Basic over-the-counter medicines

  • Pain and fever medicine
  • Antihistamine
  • Antacid
  • Antidiarrheal medicine
  • Oral rehydration packets
  • Other familiar household medicines

Read and follow labels. Check active ingredients to avoid accidental double dosing, and consider age, allergies, pregnancy, medical conditions, and drug interactions. Keep all medicine away from children.

What about antibiotics?

Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections, not every illness. Do not use leftovers casually, share prescriptions, guess the drug or dose, or use antibiotics for viral illnesses.

Any antibiotic in an emergency plan should be selected and used under appropriate medical guidance.

Free and inexpensive options

Low-Cost Solutions

  • A bag of frozen vegetables or ice wrapped in a cloth works as a cold pack for sprains — no need to buy one.
  • Clean, boiled-and-cooled water can substitute for sterile saline when rinsing minor wounds.
  • Ask a pharmacist in advance about a simple homemade oral-rehydration recipe as a backup to store-bought packets.
  • A basic drugstore first-aid kit is inexpensive and covers most of the essential supplies listed here.
  • Local fire departments, Red Cross chapters, and community centers often offer free or low-cost first-aid, CPR, and AED training.

Avoid these

Common mistakes

  • Delay calling during a life-threatening emergency.
  • Repeatedly remove pressure from a severely bleeding wound.
  • Put ice, butter, or grease on burns.
  • Move someone unnecessarily when spinal injury is suspected.
  • Give medicine without knowing what it is or checking the label.
  • Share prescription medicine.
  • Forget daily medications and medical equipment.
  • Wait until medicine runs out to seek a replacement.

Try this now

First-aid practice

Open the kit rather than simply looking at the box.

  1. Find the gloves, gauze, and wound dressings.
  2. Explain how you would call for help and control bleeding.
  3. Check every medicine and sterile item for damage or expiration.
  4. Find the paper medication and allergy lists.
  5. Identify the nearest AED and who knows CPR.
  6. List each household member’s essential medical needs.

Replace missing or expired supplies, then schedule hands-on first-aid, CPR, AED, and bleeding-control training.

Before an emergency

Quick checklist

  • I have a usable, regularly checked first-aid kit.
  • I know how to call for help and control serious bleeding.
  • I know basic wound and burn care.
  • I have medication, allergy, condition, and contact lists.
  • Essential prescriptions and equipment are in the plan.
  • I know the exact requirements for refrigerated medicines.
  • I know where the nearest AED is.
  • At least one person has current first-aid and CPR training.
