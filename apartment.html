If you live in an apartment

Apartments bring special challenges in an emergency: shared walls, shared pipes, stairs instead of yards, and less storage. This page adds apartment steps to the 12 basics. Everything else in the guide still applies.

1Water

Many apartment buildings use electric pumps to push water to upper floors. When the power goes out, upper floors can lose water first.

  • Store at least 1 gallon per person per day. Aim for at least 3 days, and 2 weeks if you have the room (FEMA).
  • When a storm or outage is coming, fill your bathtub, buckets and large pots. Use that water for flushing and washing. Do not drink it unless you treat it first.
  • Learn where your unit’s water shutoff valve is. Ask building management if you cannot find it.
  • For how to store and treat water, see the Water page.
How long can you stay without water?

No rule gives an exact number of hours. In practice it depends on stored water and working toilets.

  • With no stored water and no backup toilet: a building becomes hard to live in within about 1 to 3 days, mostly because toilets stop working.
  • With 1 gallon per person per day stored and a bucket toilet ready: many people can stay a week or more.
  • Leave if officials order an evacuation or tell you the water is unsafe.

Based on reports from a study of high-rise residents and a real high-rise evacuation in Atlanta. Treat it as a rough guide.

2Storage in small spaces

A 7-day supply takes room, and apartments have little to spare.

  • Use space under beds, at the back of closets and above cabinets.
  • Flat, stackable water containers fit tight spaces better than round jugs.
  • Keep your kit in 2 or 3 bins that one person can carry.
  • Sprouting needs only a jar and a little counter space. See Sprouting.
  • Store supplies away from heat, such as sunny windows and stoves.

3Stairs and elevators

Elevators stop working without power. In a fire they may shut down or be taken over by firefighters.

  • In a fire or other emergency, use the stairs. Do not use the elevator unless firefighters tell you to.
  • Learn where every exit stairway on your floor is, in case one is blocked.
  • In stairwells, keep to the right so emergency workers can come up.
  • Keep a flashlight and sturdy shoes by your door. Stairwells can be dark.
  • Plan how you would carry water and supplies upstairs if the elevator is off. Smaller containers are easier.

4If you cannot use the stairs

Older adults, people with disabilities and anyone recovering from illness may not be able to walk down many flights.

  • Make a plan now. Tell building management and one or two neighbors.
  • If you cannot get out, call 911, say exactly where you are, and be ready to shelter in place.
  • To shelter in place, go into a room, close the door, and seal the cracks around the door with wet towels.
  • Keep your kit, medicines and phone charger in the room where you would shelter.

5Fire safety

  • If there is a fire, leave and close your apartment door behind you. Pull the fire alarm on your way out.
  • Before opening a door, feel it with the back of your hand. If it is hot, use another way out.
  • If smoke or fire blocks the hallway, stay inside. Stuff wet towels around the door and vents, call 911 and tell them where you are.
  • Signal from a window with a flashlight or a bright cloth.
  • Once you are out, stay out.
  • Never prop stairway doors open or block exits.

6Cooking and power without danger

Generators, grills and camp stoves give off carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell that can kill without warning.

  • Never use a generator, grill or camp stove inside your home or on an enclosed balcony.
  • Generators must be outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. Most apartment balconies cannot meet that.
  • Many leases and fire rules also ban grills and open flames on balconies. Check yours.
  • Plan for food that needs no cooking. See the 30-Day Food Plan.
  • For light and phone charging, use batteries, power banks or small solar chargers.
  • Install a carbon monoxide alarm that runs on batteries or has battery backup.

7Toilets and sanitation

In an apartment building, everyone shares the same drain pipes. If the sewer backs up, toilets on lower floors can overflow.

  • If authorities say the water or sewer system is damaged, stop flushing.
  • Use the bucket-toilet method on the Sanitation page.
  • Keep heavy-duty trash bags, a bucket with a tight lid, and cat litter or sawdust on hand.
  • Ground-floor residents may want to ask management about backflow protection.

8Know your building

Ask your building manager or landlord:

  • Where are the exits, stairwells and fire alarms?
  • Is there an evacuation plan posted, and are there fire drills?
  • Does the building have a backup generator? What does it run (lights, one elevator, water pumps)?
  • Where are the water and electric shutoffs?
  • Who do residents call in an emergency after hours?
  • Also consider renter’s insurance. A landlord’s policy usually covers the building, not your belongings.

9Neighbors

In a big building, your nearest help is down the hall.

  • Swap phone numbers with one or two neighbors.
  • Agree to check on each other during outages and storms.
  • Know who on your floor lives alone, is elderly or has a disability, and might need help.

10Floors and evacuation

A high floor protects you from rising water but not from wind, broken windows, or loss of power and water. The building can also be cut off if the ground floor and streets flood.

  • Look up your evacuation zone on your county or city emergency management website.
  • If officials order your zone to evacuate, go, even if you live on a high floor.
  • During high winds, stay away from windows and glass, and move to an inner room or hallway.

Quick checklist

  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day, for at least 3 days (2 weeks if you can)
  • Fill the tub and pots before a storm
  • Know two exit stairways
  • Flashlight and shoes by the door
  • Plan for anyone who cannot use the stairs
  • No generators, grills or stoves indoors or on balconies
  • Carbon monoxide alarm with batteries
  • Bucket-toilet supplies
  • Building manager’s emergency number
  • Neighbor contact numbers
  • Know your evacuation zone
