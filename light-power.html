Light, Power & Communication

See. Charge. Listen. Stay Connected.

When electricity goes out, lights, phones, internet, refrigeration, heating controls, medical devices, and communication systems can fail together. A layered backup plan can make a major difference.

Have more than one way to see, power, and communicate.

When the power fails

Stabilize the first ten minutes

  1. 1

    Turn on a flashlight or headlamp before moving through dark rooms.

  2. 2

    Check for immediate hazards, medical-device needs, official alerts, and extreme indoor temperatures.

  3. 3

    Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed.

  4. 4

    Disconnect sensitive electronics if surges are possible, and begin conserving phone power.

  5. 5

    Tell household members what happened, where supplies are, and when the next update will be.

The essentials

Key Points

  • Battery-powered lights are generally safer than candles — never leave an open flame unattended or burning while people sleep.
  • Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed during an outage, and disconnect sensitive electronics if surges are possible.
  • Never run a generator indoors, in a garage, basement, shed, or carport — keep it at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and other openings with exhaust pointed away.
  • Reserve phone battery for emergency calls, texts, and official alerts, and use low-power settings to conserve it.
  • Choose two meeting places and one out-of-area contact, and keep the plan written on paper, not only in a phone.
  • A battery or hand-crank radio can carry weather and emergency information when phones and internet fail.

What to do

Step-by-Step Instructions

Make safe movement possible

Keep flashlights, headlamps, lanterns, and matching spare batteries easy to reach. Headlamps leave both hands free for stairs, repairs, first aid, and carrying supplies.

Reach it before standing in the dark.

Location twoNear main exits

Find shoes, keys, and a safe way out.

Battery-powered lights are generally safer than candles. Never leave any open flame unattended or burning while people sleep.

Generator safety

At least 20 feet outdoors

Keep generators away from windows, doors, attached garages, and other building openings. Point exhaust away from occupied buildings.

  • Run a generator inside a house, garage, basement, shed, or carport.
  • Connect it directly to household wiring without approved transfer equipment installed by a qualified electrician.
  • Operate it in rain, flooding, or other wet conditions.
  • Refuel a hot or running generator.
  • Use it without working carbon-monoxide alarms.

Read the manual, calculate loads, store fuel safely, and practice operation before an emergency.

Meeting and contact plan

Choose one familiar meeting place near home and a second place outside the immediate area. Select a trusted out-of-area contact who can receive each person’s location and status.

Texting may work when calls do not

During network congestion, texts may go through when voice calls do not, although delivery can be delayed. Limit non-emergency calls and do not repeatedly redial.

SAFE. AT HOME. EVERYONE OK.

LEAVING NOW. GOING TO MEETING PLACE 2.

NEED HELP. AT [LOCATION]. [SHORT PROBLEM].

Call 911 only for emergencies. Do not text or operate a phone while driving.

Community communication

Establish one known information point: a bulletin board, central room, designated radio operator, or scheduled briefing location.

  • Everyone knows where verified updates are posted.
  • One person or small team confirms information before sharing it.
  • The next update time is clearly posted.
  • People know how to report injuries, hazards, shortages, and missing persons.
  • Old notices are dated and removed to prevent confusion.

Set a recharge routine

Check power banks and rechargeable batteries on a repeating calendar date. Label each with its cable, capacity, and last-check date.

Conserve limited battery power

Reserve the phone for emergency calls, important texts, weather, official alerts, maps, and essential coordination.

  • Lower screen brightness.
  • Use low-power mode.
  • Stop video streaming.
  • Close unnecessary apps.
  • Disable unneeded background activity.
  • Use airplane mode when no connection is needed.

Free and inexpensive options

Low-Cost Solutions

  • Check whether your local library lends hand-crank radios, flashlights, or power banks before buying new.
  • Use chargers, cables, and battery packs you already own instead of purchasing specialized equipment.
  • Print contact and meeting plans at home or at a library instead of paying for a printing service.
  • Practice the one-hour outage drill with what you already have on hand — it costs nothing and reveals real gaps.
  • Keep an old working phone charged as a backup flashlight or offline information source at no extra cost.

Avoid these

Common mistakes

  • Depend on one flashlight or one charging method.
  • Leave spare batteries untested and power banks discharged.
  • Depend entirely on cell phones or the internet.
  • Run a generator indoors or near building openings.
  • Waste limited power on nonessential entertainment.
  • Keep important contacts only in a phone.
  • Assume family members know where to meet.
  • Spread information that has not been verified.

Try this now

One-hour power-outage practice

One evening, turn off normal lights for one hour without disabling safety systems or essential equipment.

  1. Have everyone find a working light.
  2. Check batteries and charge a phone from backup power.
  3. Tune the radio to a known local station.
  4. Use the printed contact and meeting plan.
  5. Identify which equipment truly requires electricity.
  6. Test one short status message to the out-of-area contact.

Correct anything that was difficult to find, discharged, unlabeled, or unknown.

Before an emergency

Quick checklist

  • I have working lights beside beds and near exits.
  • I have tested spare batteries and charged power banks.
  • I have more than one phone-charging method.
  • I have a working radio and know local stations.
  • Important contacts and instructions exist on paper.
  • Everyone knows both meeting locations.
  • We have an out-of-area contact.
  • I have listed equipment that needs priority power.
  • I know how to operate any generator safely.

Remember

Light helps people move safely. Power should be saved for what matters most.

Communication should never depend on only one system.
